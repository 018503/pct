# Battling Guide

Source: [VGCguide.com](https://www.vgcguide.com) — Battling section (17 pages). Primary authors: Aaron Traylor, Wolfe Glick.

## Core Concept: Pressure

Pokemon differs from traditional turn-based games because both players choose moves simultaneously, then watch the turn play out.

- **Proactive actions** advance winning objectives: damage, knockouts, speed control, stat boosts, status. Minimal downside unless they fail.
- **Reactive actions** respond to threats: switching, Protecting, healing. Necessary when threatened with significant setbacks.
- **Pressure** is the threat of proactive action. When Pikachu threatens to KO Gyarados with Thunderbolt, Pikachu applies pressure.

### Five Questions to Identify Pressure

1. Which Pokemon moves first? What's its likely action and field impact?
2. Which Pokemon threaten knockouts?
3. Can both opponent Pokemon coordinate for knockouts?
4. Are status moves threatening both opponents?
5. If each Pokemon achieves its primary goal, who's favored afterward?

### Thinking Sequence

1. Identify all pressure sources (threats to you and from you)
2. Evaluate severity of threats
3. Determine most likely opponent responses
4. Select proactive moves best positioned against predicted choices
5. Consider "what does the next turn look like?" before committing

## Predictions

Two categories:
1. **Comprehensive anticipation** — foreseeing every possible combination of opponent moves
2. **Specific prediction** — identifying one particular play and reacting to it

Newer players over-focus on calling exact opponent moves without considering risk-reward.

### When to Predict

| Game State | Prediction Risk | Guideline |
|---|---|---|
| **Ahead** | Low | Correct prediction wins outright; wrong prediction doesn't immediately lose |
| **Neutral** | Medium | Should swing the battle significantly in your favor |
| **Behind** | Necessary | Pursue when lacking safe plays or needing opponent mistakes |

### Safe Plays
Moves that cover multiple opponent options simultaneously. Build teams that reduce prediction necessity through superior coverage and flexibility. "Team-building/selection is critical because superior teams reduce the amount of predicting that you need to do."

## Protect — In Battle

### When to Protect

- Preventing KOs on your Pokemon
- Avoiding Fake Out on turn 1
- Keeping key Pokemon healthy
- Safe repositioning (Protect one, switch the other)
- Stalling Dynamax turns
- Stalling speed control (Trick Room, Tailwind)
- Stalling field effects (Terrain, screens, weather)
- Allowing partner to handle threats or switch safely

### When NOT to Protect

- Opponent can switch freely and improve position
- Opponent can establish speed control
- Opponent can set up stat boosts
- Opponent can double-target your partner
- You used Protect last turn (only 1/3 success rate on consecutive Protects)

### Critical Principle
"If your Pokemon is still at risk of getting knocked out the subsequent turn, Protect may not actually help with the root cause." Always ask: "If my Protect plan works, what does the next turn look like?"

### Forcing Protect
Deliberately attacking into suspected Protect. If they don't Protect, massive damage. If they do, they face low consecutive odds next turn.

### Protect Usage Stats
Learn which Pokemon typically carry Protect: only 5% of Incineroar run Protect, while 97% of Zacian do. Attack more confidently into Pokemon that rarely carry it.

## Switching

### Four Reasons to Switch

1. **Survival** — remove a threatened Pokemon from danger
2. **Positioning** — set up favorable matchups for the following turn
3. **Field conditions** — capitalize on temporary effects (Tailwind, Terrain) by switching in damage dealers
4. **Resource preservation** — protect crucial team members until opponent resources diminish

### Risks of Switching

- Opponent may not make the predicted play
- Switch-ins receive incoming attacks
- Secondary effects (crits, burns, freeze) disproportionately affect switch-ins
- You may switch into an equally unfavorable position

Evaluate two turns ahead before switching. Calculate worst-case scenarios. Verify the incoming Pokemon meaningfully improves your situation.

## Game Plans

A game plan is a general approach to how you plan on winning a battle. Five core components:

1. **Team strengths and strategies** — including specific EV/move synergies
2. **Team nuances** — the more you understand the team, the easier it will be
3. **Opponent analysis** — key Pokemon, viable counters, whether opponent recognizes solutions
4. **Win condition identification** — primary victory pathways
5. **Team preview strategy** — best four and lead, then theorize first two turns

### Battle Pace Assessment
"When you lead, are you going to have offensive pressure?" What does the end of the battle look like in your head? Avoid relying on low-attack support Pokemon as final team members.

### Turn 1
"The first turn often sets the scene for the rest of the game." Mentally simulate turn one against expected leads. Develop safe plays that succeed regardless of opponent choice.

## Team Preview

### Aaron Zheng's Three-Step Framework

1. **Understand opponent strategy** — identify synergistic combinations, speed profile, and "1v4" Pokemon (a single Pokemon capable of sweeping your whole team)
2. **Pokemon selection** — work backwards by eliminating non-contributors based on offensive typing, defensive typing, speed mismatch, combo dependency. Then identify must-haves.
3. **Lead selection** — consider opponent's most probable leads, whether your obvious combos can reliably lead, your lead's purpose, and how back-row supports if matchup goes poorly

### Narrative-Building Approach

1. Map pressure relationships (which of your Pokemon most challenge their team and vice versa)
2. Project lead scenarios from opponent's perspective
3. Identify problematic leads
4. Plan endgame states working backwards (e.g. "if I remove their Fire-type early, does my Ferrothorn become unbeatable late?")
5. Define which Pokemon cannot afford early elimination

## Opponent Analysis — Eight Dimensions

1. **Strong Pokemon** — which are their biggest threats?
2. **Speed control** — Trick Room, Tailwind, Electroweb, Icy Wind, Thunder Wave?
3. **Team pace/playstyle** — hyper offense, bulky offense, or defensive?
4. **Overall speed** — fast or slow overall?
5. **Type coverage** — does your offensive typing exploit vulnerabilities?
6. **Pokemon pairs** — which combos suggest specific leads?
7. **Support/offense ratio** — how many are support vs. attackers?
8. **Item distribution** — when multiple Pokemon typically share an item, deduce who has it

## Battling Against Trick Room

Two foundational questions:
1. Can I stop Trick Room from being set up?
2. How damaging will Trick Room be if it activates?

Trick Room lasts 5 turns (effectively 4 — the setting turn counts). The sweeper gets roughly 4 turns to deal damage.

### Time-Wasting Strategies

- Use Protect on both Pokemon alternately to consume TR turns
- Make defensive switches when predicting specific moves
- Deploy status moves strategically
- Use Fake Out when opportunities arise
- Focus on smart positioning rather than forcing knockouts

### The Indeedee Lesson
Instead of KOing the support Pokemon immediately, leaving it alive forces the opponent into difficult decisions: keep it in (susceptible to Protect stalls) or switch it out (allowing damage to the incoming sweeper). Either way, you reduce available TR turns for the sweeper from 4 to 3 or fewer.

"Pokemon is in many ways a game about making your opponent make difficult decisions."

## 1 HP Is Infinitely More Than 0 HP

A Pokemon's damage output is identical regardless of health. A 1 HP Pokemon hits just as hard as a full-health Pokemon.

### Three-Step Evaluation for Low-HP Pokemon

1. **Can I bring it back safely?** — after a teammate faints, via pivot moves (U-Turn, Volt Switch), into moves causing minimal damage
2. **Will it impact the battle meaningfully?** — can it outspeed? priority moves? useful abilities (Intimidate, weather)?
3. **Is the value worth the switching risk?**

### Sacrificial Value
- Reactivating switch-in abilities on teammates
- Enabling safe teammate switching
- Resetting teammate stat drops
- Stalling opponent field effects

## Building a Knowledge Base

Focus on roughly the top 20 most-used Pokemon rather than the entire format.

### Move Knowledge by Usage

| Usage | Approach |
|---|---|
| **44%+** | Assume the Pokemon carries it until proven otherwise |
| **10–44%** | Monitor without actively planning around |
| **Below 10%** | Don't allocate preparation; adapt in-battle |

Remember: a Pokemon can only have four moves. When opponents use uncommon attacks, they sacrifice standard coverage — exploit those gaps.

Use the 10% threshold for items too: assume common items, confirm through observation.

For EV spreads, focus on macro patterns: typical speed investments and strategic archetypes (fast vs. bulky).

## How to Analyze a Battle

Post-battle analysis is the primary mechanism for improvement.

### Priority Order

1. The turn that decided the match
2. Turns where you didn't make optimal plays or failed predictions
3. Overall Pokemon selection effectiveness
4. Lead matchup alternatives
5. Overall matchup analysis

### Turn-by-Turn Questions

- Did I make the objectively best play regardless of opponent options?
- Did I anticipate this opponent action as a possibility?
- If I'd known their exact play, would I repeat my choice?
- Was my play unnecessarily risky, even if successful?

### Key Principle
Separate successful plays from correct plays. "Just because a play didn't work out doesn't necessarily make it incorrect (and vice versa)."

### Red Flags for Teambuilding Problems (Not Play Problems)
- Consistent feeling of helplessness across multiple games
- Constant prediction necessity against specific Pokemon
- Inability to damage certain threats
- Lack of neutral leads
- Overwhelming feeling despite believing you're playing well

## Lessons from Annotated High-Level Matches

### Will Tansley vs Nils Dunlop (Worlds 2017)
- **Limiting opponent options** — "How can you limit your opponent's options so that even if they predict, they can't break through?"
- **Zero-risk plays** — ensuring your Pokemon survives predicted attacks removes opponent prediction advantage
- **Excessive double switching** — "typically a sign that you're really in trouble, because you're not doing any damage"
- **Information preservation in Bo3** — keeping Pokemon unrevealed gives less information for future games

### Paul Chua vs Diana Bros (NAIC 2019)
- **Momentum through sacrifice** — "Losing a Pokemon is not always worst — sometimes the next turn looks very very good"
- **Surprise factor** — unexpected items (Assault Vest Rayquaza) force complete damage recalculation
- **Terrain management** — using Protect to stall through field effects without opponent counterplay
- **Free switches** — threatening a KO on one Pokemon provides a free switch on the other side

### Alister Sandover vs Edoardo Ferraris (Player's Cup 1)
- **Delayed Dynamax** — conserving for optimal moments rather than using immediately is often stronger
- **Pinning** — situations where your Pokemon deals guaranteed damage regardless of opponent switches
- **"There are very few true 50-50 situations"** — most seemingly 50-50 turns have subtle information that favors one option
- **Dual clock management** — tracking Dynamax duration and Trick Room duration simultaneously
