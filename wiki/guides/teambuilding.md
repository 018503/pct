# Teambuilding Guide

Source: [VGCguide.com](https://www.vgcguide.com) — Teambuilding section (35 pages). Primary authors: Wolfe Glick (2016 World Champion), Aaron Traylor, Aaron Zheng.

## Core Principle: Intent

Every teambuilding decision should have an articulable purpose. When you add a Pokemon, pick a move, or tweak EVs, you should be able to state WHY. Track intent so you can evaluate: "Did this Pokemon do what I wanted it to do? Why or why not?"

When intent is unclear, more thought is needed.

## Starting a Team

Multiple valid starting points:

1. **Something strong** — Pokemon/strategies that are hard to play against right now
2. **The metagame** — play what's standard and modify slightly
3. **Content creators** — watch others, use rental codes to learn
4. **Collaboration** — build on someone else's idea
5. **Past formats** — Pokemon strong before that are currently underused
6. **"I can do it better"** — iterate on something you saw that had potential

You do NOT have to teambuild from scratch. Many competitive players use teams built by others. Aaron Zheng recommends newer players "try out more established teams" because "when you lose, it's easier to identify what went wrong."

### Reverse-Engineering Someone Else's Team

1. Create a **threatlist** — Pokemon/archetypes you expect to face
2. Analyze **Pokemon selection** — synergy, weakness coverage, distinctive roles
3. Examine **moves, abilities, items** — identify primary moves, unusual choices, combos
4. Identify **team modes** — lead combinations, backup options
5. Reassess your **threatlist** — develop matchup strategies for each threat
6. Optional: watch replays, pause at team preview and before moves to compare your thinking

## The 5-Step Teambuilding Process

### Step 1: The Main Idea
Start with a core concept (specific Pokemon or tactic). Add initial Pokemon, sketch rough EVs, consider moves and items.

### Step 2: Add to the Core
Add 2–4 supporting Pokemon through three strategies:
- **Complementary Offense** — type coverage, Speed, priority
- **Complementary Defense** — synergistic typing, Intimidate
- **Support** — Tailwind, Trick Room, Fake Out, Follow Me

Rules: "Every Pokemon must deal damage with at least one move." Add Pokemon to strengthen your main strategy, NOT to counter every other team. Early counter-picking creates incoherent teams.

### Step 3: Round Out the Team
With 3–5 core Pokemon, add 1–3 final members:
- Address difficult matchups (breadth)
- Add depth through new interactions/modes
- Add a complete new mode (2+ Pokemon in a different paradigm)

### Step 4: Finish the Details
Finalize EVs, moves, items, abilities. This is your first draft.

### Step 5: Start Testing
Evaluate assumptions through actual battles. Be willing to rebuild. Don't become attached to underperforming Pokemon.

## Synergy

### Offensive Synergy
Complementary damage types, coordinating knockouts, creating more attack opportunities. Example: Primal Groudon + Xerneas — Groudon removes Steel/Poison/Fire threats to Xerneas; Xerneas removes Kyogre/Rayquaza.

### Defensive Synergy
Tactical switching, absorbing attacks for each other. Key concept: **switchability** — the ability of one Pokemon to replace another while resisting incoming attacks.

Classic defensive cores: Fire/Water/Grass, Dragon/Fairy/Steel (Fantasy core).

### Anti-Synergy
When Pokemon interfere with each other. Examples: Tapu Fini's Misty Surge blocking Amoonguss's Spore; multiple weather/terrain setters conflicting.

## Cores and Modes

**Core** — the team foundation (2–3 Pokemon); everything else supports it.

**Mode** — a subset of Pokemon that work together distinctly from the rest. Examples:
- "Rain mode" (Politoed + Kingdra) on an otherwise non-rain team
- "Trick Room mode" as a secondary option

Modes add versatility but risk telegraphing strategy and reducing team cohesion.

**Battling Mode** — the practical 4-Pokemon set you bring to specific matchups. Diagnosing: Do the same 4 Pokemon feel necessary in specific matchups? Which Pokemon consistently perform well together?

## Speed Control

One of the most fundamental elements of competitive battling. Attacking first is an enormous advantage — a knocked-out Pokemon can't attack, flinch, or crit you.

| Type | Mechanism | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Tailwind** | Doubles team Speed for 4 turns | Best on very fast setters (Whimsicott, Talonflame) |
| **Trick Room** | Reverses Speed order for 5 turns | Most historically successful form; has -7 priority |
| **Speed Drops** | Icy Wind, Electroweb, Bulldoze | Less committal; drops reset on switching; best late game |
| **Speed Boosts** | Dragon Dance, Choice Scarf, weather abilities | Boosts persist through KOs |
| **Priority Moves** | Bypass all speed control | Trade priority for lower power |
| **Natural Speed** | Being naturally faster | Forces opponents to invest resources |

Nearly every successful team employs speed control. Many experienced players maintain at least two types on all their teams.

## Items

Four categories (remember: item clause = six unique items per team):

| Category | Examples | Best When |
|---|---|---|
| **Recovery** | Sitrus Berry, Leftovers, 33% berries | Middling-to-significant bulk |
| **Damage Output** | Life Orb, Choice Specs/Band, Weakness Policy | Bolstering a Pokemon's offensive strength |
| **Damage Mitigation** | Assault Vest, resist berries, Focus Sash, Eviolite | Increasing survival by 1+ turns |
| **Support** | Safety Goggles, Lum Berry, Light Clay | Specific situational needs |

Ask two questions: Does this item **bolster a strength**? Does it **mitigate a weakness**? Evaluate: How often does it activate? How impactful is the benefit? What's the opportunity cost?

## Protect

The single most important move in VGC doubles. Aim for ~4 of 6 Pokemon with Protect.

Pokemon that can skip it:
- Extremely bulky setters (Cresselia, Dusclops) with recovery moves
- Choice item holders
- Pokemon with pseudo-Protect moves (Fake Out, Dual Screens)

Decision checklist: If taking 50%+ damage from a chunk of the metagame, run Protect. If taking less than 50% from most attacks, recovery moves may substitute.

## Utility Moves

Nearly every team member carries 1–3 utility moves. Priority order:
1. **Protect** — positioning and tempo
2. **Speed Control** — Trick Room, Tailwind, Icy Wind
3. **Redirection** — Follow Me, Rage Powder
4. **Fake Out** — guaranteed flinch on first turn
5. **Taunt** — prevents non-damaging moves

Typical moveset structure: Protect / STAB 1 / STAB 2 or coverage / Utility or coverage.

## Trick Room

Won seven of twelve World Championships — the most historically consistent strategy.

Three key roles:
1. **Setters** — bulky (Cresselia, Dusclops, Porygon2) or offensive (Hatterene, Reuniclus)
2. **Helpers** — protect the setter via Fake Out or redirection (Incineroar, Amoonguss, Indeedee-F)
3. **Sweepers** — bulky, slow, hard-hitting; carry offensive items (Rhyperior, Torkoal, Glastrier)

Counters: Taunt, phasing moves (Roar/Dragon Tail), setting your own TR to cancel, Imprison + TR combo.

Integration spectrum: from full "hard Trick Room" to teams with only 2 Pokemon benefiting as a secondary mode.

## EV Spreads

### Simple Spreads (Start Here)
- "Max max" (252/252/4) is a valid starting point
- **Decide Speed stat FIRST** — the most important stat
- Invest in odd numbers of stats (1, 3, or 5) to avoid wasting EVs
- 252/244/4/4/4 is often better than 252/252/4 (one extra stat point)

Guidelines by role:
- **Fast/frail**: max Speed + primary attack stat
- **Midspeed**: check speed tiers, try 252 Speed or 252 HP
- **Trick Room**: max HP + primary attack stat, keep Speed low
- **Defensive/utility**: HP + defensive stats
- **Eviolite users**: max HP first to optimize multiplier

### Complex Spreads (Graduate To)
3-step process:
1. **No Compromises** — identify stats you cannot sacrifice
2. **Pick a Speed Stat** — determine which threats to outspeed
3. **Round Out** — use damage calculator for remaining allocations

Technical guidelines:
- Place nature boost on highest stat post-EVs
- Lower stats gain more from investment (diminishing returns)
- Never use neutral natures
- Verify stat changes actually affect damage calculations
- Surviving with minimal HP leaves you vulnerable to chip damage

## Team Playstyle and Pace

| Style | Avg Turns | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hyper Offense** | ~8 | Overwhelms opponents; great for Bo1 | Vulnerable to momentum swings |
| **Bulky Offense** | 8–12 | Most consistent; best at Worlds | Requires more decision-making |
| **Hyper Defense** | 10–15 | Hard to break through | Timer issues; less tournament success |

The guide authors recommend **Bulky Offense** as the most consistent approach.

## Common Pitfalls

### Sixth Pokemon Syndrome
When the final team slot must fulfill too many roles, the problem is usually the first five Pokemon. Solutions: explore more options, accept team limitations, or restructure/restart from 2–3 core Pokemon.

### "Island" Pokemon
Pokemon that opponents can safely ignore while destroying the rest of your team. Test: "Can opponents safely ignore this Pokemon without consequence?" If yes, reconsider. Every team member needs **board presence** — immediate or near-immediate impact through offense or support.

Not all defensive Pokemon are islands: Amoonguss (Spore threat), Dusclops (chip damage) cannot be safely ignored. Toxapex and Shuckle that rely on slow chip without partner support ARE islands.

### Beating a Single Pokemon
Four misconceptions: perfect KO rates are impossible in doubles (Fake Out, Follow Me exist); passive walling fails (opponents attack your partner); overpowered Pokemon resist single counters; tunnel vision creates weak teams. Pursue "good Pokemon with a good matchup" — strong performers that happen to advantage you against targeted threats.

## When to Move On

Move on from a team if:
1. You're not having fun
2. You're not in control of your battles (frequently forced into specific moves)
3. The metagame has shifted unfavorably
4. Opponents are too prepared for your strategy
5. You've tried everything and it doesn't work (likely Sixth Pokemon Syndrome)

Caution: a practiced flawed team typically outperforms a last-minute "perfect" team near tournaments.

## Building With Favorites

1. Figure out your Pokemon's strengths (stats, unique moves, speed, support capabilities)
2. Identify its place in the format (type advantages, speed comparisons, disruption)
3. Honestly assess weaknesses and develop mitigation via partners
4. Orient all remaining decisions toward maximizing your favorite's effectiveness
